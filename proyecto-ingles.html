01 · Introduction

What is Penicillin?

Penicillin is one of the most important inventions in the history of medicine. It was the first true antibiotic — a compound produced by a fungus that kills bacteria or stops their growth.

Before its discovery, common infections such as pneumonia, gonorrhea, or an infected wound could easily become fatal. Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928, didn't he? His discovery opened the door to modern medicine as we know it.

Antibiotic

A medicine that kills bacteria or stops their growth.

Fungus

Produced naturally by Penicillium notatum.

Infection

Before penicillin, even minor infections could be deadly.

02 · The Science

How does it work?

Penicillin works by interfering with the formation of the bacterial cell wall. Without a proper wall, bacteria cannot survive — especially when they try to divide.

Because human cells do not have a cell wall, the drug is selectively toxic: it harms bacteria without harming the patient. This is why penicillin and its derivatives — ampicillin, amoxicillin, methicillin — became the foundation of modern medicine.

03 · Timeline · Stage 01

50 years ago

The rise of resistance — mid-1970s

By the 1970s, penicillin had been saving lives for more than three decades. However, the bacteria were fighting back. Penicillin resistance, which had mainly been a problem inside hospitals, had spread to community-acquired infections.

The bacteria had learned to produce enzymes called beta-lactamases that destroyed the drug. This was a serious wake-up call for the medical world.

03 · Timeline · Stage 02

25 years ago

The MRSA crisis — around 2000

By the year 2000, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) had become a global pathogen. In Canada, the incidence of MRSA increased from 1% in 1995 to 8% by the end of 2000.

Penicillin and its derivatives were becoming less effective against common infections. Hospitals around the world were struggling with diseases that had once been easily treatable. MRSA became a global problem in the 1990s, didn't it?

03 · Timeline · Stage 03

Now

Crisis and the AI revolution — 2025–2026

Today, antimicrobial resistance is considered a global health emergency by the World Health Organization. The WHO estimates that by 2050, antibiotic-resistant infections will be associated with more than eight million deaths every year.

However, science is not standing still. Researchers are now using generative AI to design never-before-seen molecules that can kill resistant bacteria. Penicillin is still used today for many infections, isn't it?

03 · Timeline · Stage 04

The future

AI-designed antibiotics

The future of penicillin and antibiotics points toward a multidisciplinary approach. Scientists are combining AI with physics-based simulations to design new peptides that can kill dangerous bacteria such as E. coli. AI will help design new antibiotics, won't it?

The goal is to revitalize the antibiotic pipeline and face the growing threat of resistance. If governments and scientists do not act now, antimicrobial resistance will cause millions of deaths, won't it?

05 · Impact

Why it matters

Penicillin transformed medicine in ways that are difficult to overstate. It made possible procedures and treatments that are now routine — and it added roughly a decade to global life expectancy.

Surgery

Routine and emergency surgery, including cesarean sections and joint replacements.

Cancer treatment

Chemotherapy weakens the immune system and requires antibiotics to prevent infections.

Organ transplants

Immunosuppressed patients depend on antibiotics to control infections.

Common diseases

Strep throat, syphilis, and pneumonia are now easily treatable.

06 · Challenge

Antimicrobial resistance

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is now one of the greatest threats to global public health. It occurs when bacteria evolve mechanisms to survive the drugs that once killed them. Bacteria are becoming more resistant to antibiotics, aren't they?

Causes

  • Overuse and misuse of antibiotics in human medicine
  • Excessive use of antibiotics in agriculture and livestock
  • Lack of clean water and sanitation in some regions
  • Insufficient development of new antibiotics
4.95 M deaths associated with bacterial resistance in 2019 (global)
10 M+ projected deaths per year by 2050 if nothing changes

07 · Ethics

The ethical dimension

The history of penicillin also raises important ethical questions about access, responsibility, and equity.

Access

Penicillin is cheap today, but in some low-income countries, patients still die from treatable infections.

Responsibility

Doctors, patients, and farmers all play a role in preserving the effectiveness of antibiotics.

Innovation

New drugs are expensive to develop, and market incentives often do not reward antibiotic research.

Equity

The benefits of AI-driven drug discovery must reach all countries, not only wealthy ones.

A story still being written

Penicillin is more than a medicine. It is a symbol of human ingenuity, a turning point in medical history, and a reminder that science must keep evolving. From Fleming's accidental discovery in 1928 to the AI-driven laboratories of 2026, its story is one of adaptation, resistance, and hope.
